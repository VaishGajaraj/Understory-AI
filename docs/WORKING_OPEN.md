# Understory Open

**An open-source project to detect ground disturbance beneath forest canopy using free NISAR L-band radar — and a map of everything else the same foundation could grow into.**

## 1. The problem in one page

A large share of environmental crime happens under trees, where the world's monitoring systems cannot see. Illegal loggers in the tropics rarely clear-cut; they punch narrow access roads into intact forest, extract valuable trees selectively, and leave the canopy largely closed above them. Illegal gold miners operate the same way. By the time damage is visible from above, the operation has moved on.

Existing forest monitoring fails here for two stacked reasons. Optical satellites are blocked by cloud cover for weeks or months at a time in exactly the geographies where this happens — the Amazon, the Congo Basin, Borneo. And even on a clear day, a camera sees the tops of trees: if the leaves remain, the road beneath them is invisible. Most radar satellites solve the cloud problem but not the canopy problem, because their short wavelengths (X- and C-band) scatter off the top of the canopy rather than penetrating it.

L-band radar, with a wavelength of roughly 24 cm, passes through leaves and small branches and returns from the ground and tree trunks. This has been understood for decades, but there has never been a free, systematically repeating, global L-band data stream — until now. NISAR, the $1.5B NASA–ISRO radar mission launched in July 2025, images essentially all of Earth's land every ~12 days in L-band, and the data is entirely free and public. Fully calibrated global products began releasing in July 2026, with the full mission backlog being reprocessed and published through the end of the year. The data stream is weeks old. No open exploitation layer for forest degradation exists on it yet, and the incumbent alert systems (GLAD, RADD, DETER) publicly acknowledge that under-canopy degradation is the gap they have not solved.

## 2. How detection works

The method is repeat-pass interferometric **coherence change detection (CCD)**. Radar measures not just brightness but phase — effectively a fingerprint of how a patch of ground scattered the signal. Image the same forest twice, twelve days apart: if nothing happened, the fingerprints match (high coherence). If something physically rearranged the surface — soil compacted by vehicles, brush cleared, logs dragged, a track cut — coherence collapses in exactly that spot, even beneath closed canopy. The output is not a picture to be interpreted but a map of "the ground physically changed here between pass A and pass B."

The hard problem, and the reason this is a real research project rather than a weekend script, is that nature also breaks the fingerprint. Wind, rain, soil-moisture change, and vegetation growth all lower coherence, so a naive coherence map of a rainforest is a wall of noise. The core technical work is separating human-caused disturbance from natural decorrelation. Human activity tends to be geometrically linear (roads, trails), spatially compact, and persistent across subsequent passes; natural decorrelation is diffuse, weather-correlated, and transient. Encoding that separation — first as statistical baselines and filters, eventually as models trained on labeled ground truth — is the entire intellectual content of the project.

## 3. The project: an open benchmark, not a platform

The most common way open-source projects die is by scoping themselves as infrastructure ("a framework for SAR analysis") rather than as an answer to a question. This project is scoped as a question:

> **Can NISAR L-band coherence detect documented forest degradation events — how early, at what minimum event size, and at what false-alarm rate?**

Nobody in the world currently knows the answer on real NISAR data. Publishing it — favorable or not — is a genuine contribution.

### Deliverables

The project produces three artifacts. First, a **reproducible pipeline**: a Python repository that pulls NISAR L2 interferometric products from the Alaska Satellite Facility (`asf_search`, direct S3 access), builds coherence time-series stacks over an area of interest using open tooling (ISCE3, GDAL), models expected per-pixel seasonal coherence, and flags anomalies that persist across two or more passes with human-shaped geometry. The entire benchmark runs from a single command on a cloud VM. Second, a **published benchmark report**: precision/recall against externally documented ground truth, detection lead time versus the optical alert record for the same events, minimum detectable event size, and an honest failure analysis with maps. Third, the seed of an **open labeled library**: a simple, versioned schema for confirmed and rejected disturbance events, biome-indexed from the first entry, designed so that field partners can contribute verified labels over time.

### Test geographies

Two complementary sites. A region of the Brazilian Amazon with dated, located degradation events documented by Imazon's SAD program and IBAMA enforcement records, falling inside NISAR's archived acquisition window — this provides external ground truth no one can accuse the project of curating. And instrumented Eastern-woodland ground-truth sites with controlled disturbances of known date, size, and type — these produce the minimum-detectable-event-size curve that published records cannot.

### Success and kill criteria

Stated before building, as always. The signal is worth pursuing further if precision against external ground truth reaches roughly 70% at operationally useful recall; if events at or below ~2 hectares are detectable (larger than that, and optical systems eventually catch the clearing anyway, gutting the differentiation claim); and if median detection lead over optical alerts in cloudy-season tropics is three weeks or more. If the numbers land short, the failure analysis gets published anyway — a documented negative result on a brand-new public instrument saves everyone who follows months of effort.

One known caveat to carry honestly: pre-calibration NISAR products have documented radiometric artifacts. Any numbers generated on the early archive must be re-validated on the calibrated July 2026+ stream before being treated as final.

### Timeline and posture

Evenings-and-weekends scale: roughly 8–10 hours per week for three months to a published benchmark. Apache 2.0 license, standard repository hygiene (CONTRIBUTING, CITATION.cff, CI on toy data), decisions in the open. Launch the repository and the benchmark writeup together — the numbers are the announcement. Distribution targets: the geospatial open-source community (Pangeo, FOSS4G, r/remotesensing), and direct outreach to the handful of groups who will care most — the GFW/RADD team at Wageningen, Imazon, MAAP/Amazon Conservation, and the NISAR applications team at JPL, which is actively seeking early adopters.

### Theory of change

A detection feed only matters where someone acts on detections. The impact path runs through users, not code: one enforcement agency, indigenous territorial-monitoring program, or NGO acting on alerts converts the project from interesting to important — and simultaneously generates the first analyst-confirmed labels for the library. Recruiting one such partner with a free monitored watch area is a first-class project goal, not an afterthought. The precedent is real: GLAD alerts, built by a single university lab on free Landsat data, now underpin deforestation response globally. That is the ceiling for what a small open project on free public data can become.

### Watch item: OPERA

NASA's OPERA project produces free, analysis-ready surface-disturbance products from Sentinel-1 and NISAR. If OPERA ships an official NISAR disturbance product line, this project should build on it rather than compete with it — free government upstream processing is a gift, exactly like the free satellite itself.

## 4. Related project ideas on the same foundation

Everything below shares the same skeleton — free national radar data, an ML layer separating weak human signal from natural geophysical noise, and a compounding labeled library — and most share large parts of the same codebase. They are listed in rough order of adjacency to the core project.

**Illegal mining detection.** Artisanal and illegal gold mining under canopy (Amazon, Ghana, DRC) is arguably an easier coherence signal than selective logging — pits, soil disturbance, and sediment plumes are larger and more persistent — with motivated NGO, journalistic, and government users. This is less a separate project than a second benchmark geography for the same pipeline, and a natural second chapter for the open library.

**EUDR compliance signal (the commercial track).** The EU Deforestation Regulation becomes enforceable for large and medium operators on 30 December 2026 and explicitly requires demonstrating freedom from forest *degradation*, which incumbent optical stacks admit they cannot systematically measure. The commercial expression of this project is an API and evidence service selling under-canopy degradation events to the compliance platforms that own customer workflows. The open pipeline and the proprietary business split cleanly: pipeline and coherence engine open, labeled library, biome noise models, and compliance-grade evidence service closed. Starting open forecloses nothing here — it commoditizes the layer where no moat exists anyway and builds the credibility and distribution a later company would want.

**Carbon MRV degradation monitoring.** Forest-carbon registries and raters, under credibility pressure, increasingly need independent evidence of *degradation* rather than only deforestation. The output format is identical — ranked disturbance events over subscribed polygons — and the evidence-pack artifact maps directly to audit documentation. A plausible second commercial vertical, or an early adopter community for the open feed.

**Open labeled disturbance library as a standalone contribution.** Potentially the most durable artifact of all: a public, biome-indexed, ground-truthed dataset of "human disturbance vs. natural decorrelation" coherence signatures, with a contribution schema for field-verified labels. Open data of this kind tends to outlive any single pipeline, becomes the standard benchmark others must cite, and — in the commercial framing — the curated superset remains the defensible asset.

**Further afield, same skillset, different physics.** Adjacent NISAR applications that trade coherence magnitude for interferometric phase, listed for completeness rather than near-term pursuit: groundwater-compliance subsidence monitoring (SGMA-driven, nearly uncontested, slow public-sector buyers), permafrost-thaw infrastructure risk (empty niche, small buyer pool), and tailings-dam deformation (real GISTM mandate, but a saturated incumbent landscape and a 20-year head start on atmospheric correction — a data-supplier play at best). These are noted as horizon options; each would be a genuinely different processing stack and should not dilute the core benchmark.

## 5. Other ideas from this exploration, same analytical frame

These came out of the same evaluation exercise — each was filtered through the same tests (commodity data input, software-only hard part, compounding labeled library, named buyer within twelve months, ideally a regulation forcing the purchase) — but they sit farther from the core forest project, either because the physics differs or because the buyer does. They are recorded here so the thinking isn't lost, roughly in the order they ranked.

**Tailings-dam and mine-infrastructure deformation monitoring.** After the Brumadinho disaster, the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management (GISTM) pushed miners, insurers, and investors toward independent monitoring of thousands of tailings facilities, many in remote or cloudy geographies. Detecting slow structural creep before failure is a textbook weak-signal-in-natural-noise problem — millimeter deformation against seasonal moisture swelling and atmospheric artifacts. Ranked second in our evaluation but demoted from the wedge position for a hard reason: the major miners already run enterprise-scale InSAR programs served by established vendors (3vGeomatics, GroundProbe, IDS GeoRadar, Fugro, SkyGeo), so the procurement cycle has largely been won, and the technique (interferometric phase) is a genuinely different and harder stack than coherence magnitude. Best treated as a possible second vertical or data-supplier play, never the entry point.

**Groundwater-compliance subsidence monitoring.** California's SGMA requires groundwater sustainability agencies to monitor and report land subsidence, and comparable aquifer stress exists across the American Southwest, India, and elsewhere. Over-pumping subsidence is a slow, wide-area signal — well matched to a 12-day revisit and wide swath, poorly matched to expensive tasked commercial imagery — and the competitive field is nearly empty. The tempering factors: public-sector buyers move slowly, budgets are thin, and there is no hard deadline doing the selling. A patient, uncontested niche rather than a sprint.

**Permafrost-thaw infrastructure risk.** Pipelines, rail, airstrips, and buildings across Alaska, Canada, and the Nordics sit on measurably deforming ground as permafrost degrades — a seasonal-noise-versus-structural-trend separation problem almost custom-built for L-band time series, with buyers in regulated pipeline integrity-management programs and insurance. Nearly empty niche, but a small near-term buyer pool and no forcing deadline.

**Utility and pipeline right-of-way encroachment.** Vegetation change and ground disturbance along transmission and pipeline corridors. Proven budgets exist — LiveEO built its original business here — which cuts both ways: demand is validated, and an incumbent already owns the workflow. The realistic shape is supplying a differentiated radar signal into their platform rather than competing for the end customer.

**Construction and infrastructure "proof of progress" for lenders.** Private credit and infrastructure funds lend against remote projects they currently verify through site visits and self-reported photos; a persistent change-detection feed (earthworks started, structures appearing, activity stalled) is pattern-of-life analytics for capital deployment. The cautionary history: Orbital Insight and SpaceKnow partially burned this ground with hedge-fund-alpha positioning. The lesson, if ever pursued, is to sell it as loan-covenant verification — a compliance workflow — rather than a trading signal.

**Parametric insurance triggers from soil moisture.** Drought and flood parametric products need an objective, tamper-proof index to trigger payouts, and L-band soil-moisture measurement works precisely where optical fails — monsoon-season cloud cover, which is why the India angle is structurally interesting given ISRO co-owns the mission. Ranked last among the commercial ideas: the buyers (reinsurers, insurtechs) are hard to reach, and incumbent weather-index and soil-water products are better established.

**Timberland asset monitoring.** Verifying that nothing is happening on remote holdings for TIMOs and timber REITs — a small market, but sticky recurring revenue, and trivially the same product as the core forest feed pointed at a different polygon owner. More a future customer segment for the open project's commercial expression than a distinct idea.

A pattern worth preserving across all of these: the deformation-based ideas (tailings, subsidence, permafrost) use interferometric *phase* rather than coherence magnitude — technically adjacent but a genuinely different processing stack with a steeper accuracy bar and incumbents who have spent two decades on atmospheric correction. The coherence-based ideas (everything forest-related, right-of-way, construction activity) share the core project's codebase almost entirely. Adjacency should be measured in code shared, not in satellite shared. And one boundary to hold by design rather than by accident: pattern-of-life framing drifts quickly toward monitoring people rather than land; everything here stays pointed at terrain, structures, and vegetation.

## 6. Consolidating this into one open-source project

The ideas above could fragment into a dozen repos and die of dispersion. The consolidation move is to recognize that nearly everything coherence-based shares one skeleton, and to build that skeleton once as a small family of packages under a single project umbrella — "Understory" as the organization, with clearly separated layers:

**`understory-core`** — the data plumbing. NISAR granule discovery and retrieval, co-registration, coherence stack construction, tiling, and caching. This layer is application-agnostic: the same code serves a forest in Pará, a right-of-way in Texas, or a construction site anywhere. It should be the most boring, best-tested code in the project, because everything sits on it and because it's the layer most likely to attract outside contributors (many people want NISAR plumbing; few care about your classifier).

**`understory-detect`** — the science. Seasonal coherence baseline models, anomaly detection, persistence and geometry filters, and the scoring harness that evaluates any detector against any labeled event set. Designing the detector as a pluggable interface matters more than the first detector being good: it invites researchers to submit competing methods against the same benchmark, which is how open benchmarks become community standards rather than one person's repo.

**`understory-labels`** — the dataset, versioned separately from all code. A schema for disturbance events (geometry, date window, class, confirmation status, evidence source, biome), published as versioned releases with a changelog, and a contribution path for field-verified labels. Kept deliberately separate because open data outlives any pipeline, and because its governance needs are different — label quality control is editorial work, not code review.

**Application layers on top** — thin, and added only when a real user exists: the forest-degradation benchmark configuration first, a mining configuration second, others only on demand. The discipline that keeps the project alive: no application layer without a named user, and no feature in core that only one application needs.

Practical choices worth fixing early because they're expensive to change: Apache 2.0 across code; CC-BY 4.0 for the label dataset; Python throughout; a monorepo initially (splitting later is easy, unifying later is not); a GitHub organization from day one rather than a personal repo; CI that runs the full pipeline on a checked-in toy granule so that "does it still work" is never a matter of memory; and a versioned methodology document alongside the code, because for a benchmark project the *method* is the citable artifact and reviewers must be able to point at a frozen version.

Governance stays simple — single maintainer, decisions in public issues — with one exception designed in from the start: label contributions get a documented review standard (what counts as "confirmed," what evidence is required) even when the reviewer is just you, because the labeled library is the asset whose integrity matters most, and retrofitting rigor onto a contaminated dataset is nearly impossible.

## 7. Technical specification, going in

What follows is the concrete stack as best it can be specified before contact with the data, with the honest expectation that first contact will revise it.

**Input data.** NISAR Level-2 products from the Alaska Satellite Facility DAAC: the interferometric product line (which carries the coherence layer directly) as the primary input, with GCOV (geocoded covariance/backscatter) as a secondary channel for corroborating features. Discovery and retrieval via the `asf_search` Python client; the data resides in AWS cloud storage, so the pipeline should be built to run in-region with direct S3 access rather than downloading — the archive is already hundreds of terabytes and a download-based design punishes every experiment. Auxiliary inputs: ERA5 reanalysis for precipitation and wind joins (natural decorrelation is weather-correlated, and the baseline model needs to know what the weather was), a forest/land-cover mask (ESA WorldCover or similar) to constrain the search space, and a Copernicus DEM where terrain normalization is needed.

**Processing chain.** Per track and frame: ingest consecutive-pass interferometric pairs, extract coherence rasters, and stack them into a per-pixel time series aligned on the 12-day repeat cycle. Where custom interferometry is needed beyond the delivered products, ISCE3 is the reference open toolchain; GDAL/rasterio for all raster handling; Xarray with Dask for the stack computations, which parallelize naturally per tile. Storage formats: cloud-optimized GeoTIFF for rasters, GeoParquet for event tables, and STAC metadata over everything, so the project's own outputs are discoverable with the same tooling as its inputs.

**The baseline model (v0).** For each pixel or small superpixel, model expected coherence as a function of season, land-cover class, local incidence angle, and recent precipitation — starting deliberately simple, with a rolling robust mean and variance per pixel per season, refined only where the benchmark shows simplicity failing. A disturbance candidate is coherence below the expected envelope by a tunable threshold.

**The detector (v0).** Three filters applied to candidates, in order of cheapness: persistence (the anomaly recurs across two or more consecutive pairs — the single most powerful false-alarm cut available at zero model complexity), spatial clustering (minimum contiguous area), and geometry (elongation and linearity scoring to favor road- and trail-shaped features). Machine learning enters only at v1, once the benchmark exists to measure whether it beats the filters — a gradient-boosted model over engineered features (anomaly depth, persistence length, shape metrics, weather context) trained on the labeled library is the likely first step. Resisting the urge to start with deep learning is a design decision: the project's credibility rests on a legible method that a skeptical remote-sensing reviewer can audit.

**The scoring harness.** Given any detector and any labeled event set, produce: event-level precision, recall, and F1 (with spatial and temporal matching tolerances stated explicitly in the methodology document), detection latency versus event date, detection lead versus the optical alert record for the same events, and minimum-detectable-size curves. Every benchmark run emits a machine-readable report; the published tables are generated, never hand-assembled.

**Compute and cost.** A single mid-size cloud VM in the data's home region with a few hundred gigabytes of scratch is sufficient for a two-geography benchmark; the design target is that a full benchmark run costs tens of dollars, not thousands, because a benchmark nobody can afford to reproduce is not a benchmark. GPU is unnecessary until v1 modeling, and possibly not then.

**Known technical risks, stated going in.** Pre-calibration artifacts in the current archive (radiometric banding — re-validation on calibrated data is a mandatory gate, not an option); ionospheric noise, to which L-band is more susceptible than shorter wavelengths, partially correctable with standard split-spectrum methods; steep-terrain geometric distortion in some geographies (mask it, don't model it, in v0); and the 12-day revisit as a hard floor on detection latency — the project should never promise faster than the physics allows.

## 8. How to actually make an impact

This is the section that matters most and gets skipped most. Code detects; only institutions respond. The theory of change has to be engineered with the same seriousness as the pipeline, and it decomposes into four problems.

**Problem one: reaching a responder.** The users who can act on an under-canopy alert already exist and already run monitoring programs — they are not hypothetical. Imazon and MAAP publish operational deforestation analyses for the Amazon and have institutional workflows for exactly this kind of signal. Indigenous territorial-monitoring programs, many supported by organizations like Rainforest Foundation US, run patrol-and-verify operations that convert alerts into field checks. National enforcement agencies are the highest-impact, hardest-to-reach tier. The realistic entry point is the middle tier: one NGO or territorial program, offered a free persistent watch area over a region *they* choose, with alerts delivered in whatever format they already use — most run QGIS or simple web maps, and matching their existing workflow beats building them a new one. The ask is small and concrete: look at our alerts for one season and tell us which ones were real. That sentence is the entire partnership pitch, and it is also — not coincidentally — the label-generation engine.

**Problem two: earning trust in the signal.** A responder's scarcest resource is field-verification effort; a false alarm costs them a boat trip. This is why the benchmark precedes the partnership: published precision numbers, an honest failure analysis, and detection-lead evidence are what make a serious organization willing to spend one verification on the feed. Impact discipline here means shipping alerts ranked and thresholded conservatively — a short list of high-confidence events beats a comprehensive noisy layer for every real user, even though the comprehensive layer demos better.

**Problem three: closing the loop, visibly.** The project should define its success metric now, publicly, and it is not stars or citations: it is **verified detections acted upon** — an alert that led to a field verification, an enforcement referral, a documented case. One such case, written up carefully (with the partner's consent, and with care not to expose operationally sensitive locations or endanger the people doing the verifying), does more for adoption than any conference talk, because it converts the project from a method into a precedent. Secondary metrics that honestly track impact: hectares under active watch for a real partner, labels contributed through field verification, and independent groups running the benchmark.

**Problem four: sustaining it past the first burst.** Solo open-source projects die at month six when novelty fades. Three structural supports help: keep the smallest maintainable scope (the benchmark and one partner feed — everything else in this document is explicitly *later*); recruit the second maintainer from the user side rather than the developer side (a partner's GIS analyst who wants a feature has more staying power than a drive-by contributor); and put the project inside an existing community's field of view early — Pangeo, FOSS4G, NISAR applications workshops — so its continuation doesn't depend on one person's evenings staying free. If the project earns traction, small grants exist for precisely this shape of work (NASA's applications programs, environmental data philanthropies, climate-focused funders), and a maintained public good with a verified-impact case study is exactly what they fund.

The quiet point underneath all four: every impact mechanism above — the partner, the conservative thresholds, the verified-case writeup, the label loop — is also the thing that would make any later commercial or grant-funded expression of this work stronger. Impact-first isn't a detour from the other paths in this document; it is their common prerequisite.

## 9. Sequencing

The order of operations that keeps every door open: write the benchmark README first (it forces the scope decisions and doubles as the outreach document to JPL, Imazon, and collaborators); build and run the benchmark on the existing archive; re-validate on calibrated data; publish repo and report together; recruit one acting user. Only then decide — with real numbers and real users in hand — whether the next move is a company, a grant, or simply a maintained public good. The open project is not the consolation prize for not starting a company; it is the correct first move in every branch of the decision tree.

## 10. Defense applications, briefly

The physics does not distinguish between a logging skid trail and any other concealed ground activity, and it is worth stating plainly — in one contained section — what that implies. The same coherence signal that reveals selective logging under canopy reveals newly cut trails, compacted routes from vehicle traffic, cleared staging areas, and emplaced structures in vegetated terrain. This addresses a standing surveillance gap: electro-optical satellites cannot see through canopy, commercial X-band SAR scatters off the top of it, and airborne foliage-penetration radar is scarce and expensive to task persistently. Missions with a natural fit include counter-narcotics (detecting trafficking infrastructure — airstrips, trails, encampments — under jungle canopy for task forces like JIATF-S), border and partner-nation security, and general persistent situational awareness in forested theaters, delivered in tactical formats such as Cursor-on-Target overlays into TAK-based systems. Because the data source is free, unclassified, and globally shared, any capability built on it is inherently coalition-shareable — attractive to defense buyers, and also the reason the capability cannot be monopolized by anyone, this project included.

Two honest observations follow. First, the open benchmark developed here would double as feasibility evidence for defense-facing funding vehicles (an SBIR Phase I is the canonical on-ramp), which means the open project subsidizes rather than forecloses that path. Second, the dual-use nature cuts both ways and deserves eyes-open treatment: publishing strong under-canopy detection methods openly means enforcement agencies, NGOs, *and* militaries — including ones you don't choose — can use them. The mitigations are structural rather than cosmetic: the method detects terrain disturbance, not people; the 12-day revisit makes it an infrastructure-and-pattern tool, not a tactical tracking one; and the project's own norms (conservative thresholds, partner consent, care with sensitive locations) set the precedent for how the capability gets used. This document keeps the defense thread deliberately separate from the civilian project's identity, partnerships, and communications — the Amazon NGO partner and the counter-narcotics sponsor should never be asked to share a brand.

## 11. Accelerator alignment

If the benchmark succeeds and the commercial expression (the EUDR degradation signal of Section 4) is pursued, the work in this document maps onto the major early-stage programs in different ways — and the fit ranking is not obvious, so it's worth spelling out.

**South Park Commons is the best fit for where this actually is today.** SPC exists precisely for the "-1 to 0" phase — technical people exploring what to build before committing to a company — and its founder-fellowship model funds exactly this shape of work: a person with a differentiated technical thesis running structured experiments toward a go/no-go. This document *is* an SPC application in substance: a novel data asset, a falsifiable benchmark, kill criteria, and multiple validated expansion paths. Applying there doesn't require pretending the company already exists.

**Y Combinator becomes the right move only after the benchmark, and the narrative writes itself if the numbers land.** The YC-legible story is one sentence: "EUDR enforcement begins December 30; importers must prove no forest degradation; degradation under canopy is invisible to every optical monitoring stack; we are the only signal that sees it, built on a free $1.5B national data asset." That is a deadline-driven wedge with a data-flywheel moat and SaaS economics — a classic YC shape. What YC will probe: single-satellite dependence (answer: the labeled library transfers to ROSE-L and successors), why incumbents won't just build it (answer: speed and the label head start — true, but it means velocity genuinely is the strategy), and founder commitment (an open-source side project reads as a strength only once it has users; before that it reads as a hobby). The open repo, if it has adoption by application time, converts from liability to traction slide: "our toolkit is the default way anyone touches this data."

**Techstars fits a different branch** — its vertical programs (sustainability-, space-, and Earth-observation-focused cohorts, often with corporate or agency partners) offer domain distribution more than brand halo. If the chosen wedge ends up being one where a specific Techstars partner owns the customer relationship (a mining or energy corporate for the adjacent verticals, an EO-sector program for the core), the mentor-network value can exceed YC's generality. The trade: smaller check, more equity-per-dollar, wildly variable program quality by city — worth it only for a named partner, not in the abstract.

The sequencing implication is the same one this document keeps arriving at: the benchmark is the universal currency. It is simultaneously the SPC exploration artifact, the YC traction evidence, the Techstars technical credential, the grant-application preliminary result, and the NGO partnership pitch. Every path forward — accelerator, grant, open public good, or company — buys its next step with the same coin, which is why the only wrong move available right now is postponing the benchmark to polish any of the destinations it unlocks.

Understory is not really a database problem in the traditional sense — it's a raster/array problem. NISAR coherence stacks are big multidimensional arrays, and the right pattern is cloud-optimized formats in object storage (Zarr or COGs on S3), processed with xarray/dask, not rows in a database. Where you do want a database is for the derived products: detection polygons, benchmark results, scene metadata, AOI definitions. That's PostGIS — again just Postgres with the spatial extension, hosted anywhere. So the pattern is: S3 + Zarr for the heavy data, PostGIS for vectors and metadata, and something like a STAC catalog if you want the open-source project to feel institutional-grade (STAC is the lingua franca for this community and would help credibility with exactly the institutional adopters your theory of change depends on).
